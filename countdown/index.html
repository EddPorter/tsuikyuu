Reaching the target

The numbers round of a well-known quiz show gives you six numbers and a target, and asks you to reach the target with the four basic operations. People do it by feel in half a minute. A computer can do it by trying everything, but there is a much smaller way: remember what every subset of the numbers can make, and build the bigger subsets from the smaller ones. This page does both and counts the work.

The game

These are the usual rules, and the ones this page uses:

  • There are six number tiles. Some are large (25, 50, 75, 100, one of each in the pool) and the rest are small (1 to 10, two of each). The contestant chooses how many of the six come from the large tiles, from none to all four.
  • A target between 101 and 999 is chosen at random.
  • Combine the numbers with +, −, × and ÷, using brackets freely. Each number may be used at most once, and you need not use all six.
  • Every step must give a positive whole number. No fractions and no negatives, even part way.

Pick the number of large tiles, or type your own numbers and target. The page solves it straight away, using the method described below.

How many large numbers

The solver only needs to find a way. When there are several, it shows one that uses the fewest numbers.

Trying everything

The most direct method mirrors what a person does. Pick any two numbers from the list. Combine them with each operation that keeps a positive whole number. Replace the pair with the result, so the list is one shorter. Then do the same again on the shorter list, and so on until one number is left. Every value that turns up along the way is one you can reach. Click “Count the work” to run it for the first 3, 4, 5 and 6 of your numbers, next to the method in the rest of this page.

Both methods are counting results of an arithmetic operation. The times depend on your device. For six numbers, trying everything is not out of reach of a computer. It takes about a million and a half steps, which is a fraction of a second. But it is slow for a person, and it grows fast. Each extra number multiplies the work by something like 25 to 70. For the numbers 100, 75, 50, 25, 6, 3 and 2, I measured 104,034,868 operations for trying everything, about 15 seconds in a standard JavaScript runtime. The method below needed 1,801,236 for the same seven numbers, about 0.2 seconds. That is a 58-fold saving, up from about 12-fold at six numbers. I did not run trying everything at eight numbers, and would not expect it to finish quickly.

The same work, over and over

Why is there so much work to save? Because different routes arrive at the same place. Take the numbers 100, 6, 75 and 3. You could add 100 and 6 first and then multiply 75 by 3. Or you could multiply 75 by 3 first, and then add 100 and 6. Both leave the same position, 106 and 225, with whatever else was left. Trying everything cannot tell, so it goes on from that position twice, and works through everything that follows twice.

The remedy is to notice what matters. When two routes leave the same numbers, what happens next does not depend on the route. In fact it is more general than that: what a group of numbers can make does not depend on the order in which you combined them.

Remember what each subset can make

Number the six tiles and think of every subset of them: 26 − 1 = 63 non-empty ones. For each subset S, work out once reach[S]: every value you can make using exactly the numbers in S.

A single number
reach of a subset holding one number is just that number.
Two or more numbers
Any way of making a value from them ends with one last operation, joining a value made from one part of S with a value made from the rest. So split S into two non-empty parts A and B in every possible way. For every value a in reach[A] and every value b in reach[B], put a and b through each operation, and add the results to reach[S].
The order of work
Both parts are smaller than S, so if we deal with subsets from the smallest to the largest, reach[A] and reach[B] are always ready. This is the same dependence on smaller answers as the grids on What changed?.

Two things save the work. Each subset is worked out only once, however many orders could lead to it. And each reach is a set, so if two different routes make the same value from the same subset, we keep it once and carry on from it once. The answer to the game is whether the target appears in reach[S] for any subset S, since you do not have to use every number.

Reading the answer back

Knowing a value is reachable is not yet an answer. You want the sum. So each value stored in reach[S] also keeps a note of how it was first made: the operation, the two values joined, and which subsets they came from. To show a solution, start at the target, look up its note, and do the same for each of the two values it was joined from, until you reach numbers that were on the tiles. That is the same trick as walking back through the grids on What changed?, and it is how the result above is written out as a list of steps and as one line with brackets.

Which split is best?

The contestant chooses how many numbers come from the large tiles, so it is fair to ask whether the choice matters. With the method above it is cheap to find out. Click the button, and the page deals many random sets of tiles for each split, as the show does, without putting tiles back. For each set it works out the share of targets from 101 to 999 that are reachable.

It is a sample of random sets. A bigger sample would move the numbers a little, and the same seeds are used each time, so you will see the same result on every run. “Reachable” means exactly reachable. In the game, getting close also scores.

What this leaves out

  • It does not race the clock. The show gives a person about half a minute. This page is about how a computer can do it without trying everything.
  • It does not pick the nicest answer. It shows the first way it found that uses the fewest numbers, which may not be the easiest to do in your head.
  • The sets get large. For six numbers the stored values number in the tens of thousands, which is fine. The memory is what limits it as numbers are added, before the time does.
  • Close answers. When the target is not reachable, the page shows the closest value it can reach. It does not model the show’s scoring for near misses.

In short

  • Trying every way to combine pairs works, but different routes keep arriving at the same position and the work is repeated.
  • What a group of numbers can make does not depend on the order they were combined in, so work it out once for each subset.
  • A subset’s values come from splitting it into two smaller subsets in every way and combining their values.
  • Keeping values in sets removes repeated values. A note with each value lets you read the sum back.
  • For six numbers the saving is about twelve times, for seven about fifty-eight. It grows with every number added.
