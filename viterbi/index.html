The most likely story

Sometimes you cannot see what is happening, only clues that depend on it. From a week of clues you want the single most likely story of what really went on. There are far too many stories to try them all, but one idea, the Viterbi algorithm, finds the best by remembering only the best way to reach each possibility at each step. This page builds it from a small weather puzzle.

The puzzle

A friend lives somewhere you cannot see the sky from. You never learn the weather. You only hear what they did each day: they walked, shopped or cleaned. The weather, though, affects what they do. It is probably sunny on a walking day and rainy on a cleaning day. Weather also tends to carry on, so a rainy day is likely to be followed by another.

So the facts, with every number made up for this page, are these:

Where it starts
The first day is rainy with chance 0.6 and sunny with chance 0.4.
How weather carries on
Each day, rain stays rain with some chance and sun stays sun with some chance. You can change both below.
What the weather makes your friend do
On a rainy day: walk 0.1, shop 0.4, clean 0.5. On a sunny day: walk 0.6, shop 0.3, clean 0.1.

A model like this, with a hidden chain of states and visible clues, is called a hidden Markov model. Given the clues for a run of days, which weather sequence is most likely?

Try it

Set what your friend did on each day. The page finds the most likely weather for the whole run, and compares it with a simpler idea: guess each day on its own from that day’s clue alone, ignoring the other days.

Most likely story (Viterbi)
Each day on its own

Try the “One odd day” example. A single cleaning day sits between walking days. On its own, a cleaning day looks rainy. But if sun carries on strongly, a week of sun with one odd day is a more likely story than two sudden changes of weather. Looking at each day alone cannot see that. It also scores each day without asking whether the whole sequence hangs together.

Too many stories

With two kinds of weather and n days there are 2n possible stories. Seven days gives 128. A real problem might have fifty states and a hundred steps. We need to avoid scoring every story.

Here is the observation that does it. Suppose the best story for the first t days ends with rain on day t. Then the part of it that covers the first t−1 days must be the best story ending in whatever weather came before. If some better way to get to that earlier day existed, we could swap it in and improve the whole. So for each day and each weather we only need to remember one thing: the best story that ends there. Everything else can be forgotten.

The grid

Draw a grid with a column for each day and a row for each kind of weather. Each cell holds the probability of the best story that ends in that weather on that day, and which weather the day before that story came from.

The first column
The chance of starting in that weather, times the chance of seeing that day’s clue from it.
Any later cell
For each weather the day before, take its cell, multiply by the chance of moving from it to this weather, and keep the best. Remember which one won. Then multiply by the chance of this day’s clue.
The answer
The largest cell in the last column. Follow the remembered choices back, column by column, to read off the whole story.

Press “Fill next cell” to fill the grid one cell at a time, going down each column in turn. The table underneath shows the sums behind the cell just filled. You can click any filled cell to see its sums instead.

A small note on numbers. Multiplying many chances makes very small numbers, and a computer can run out of precision. Real code adds logarithms of the chances instead of multiplying them, which gives the same ordering. This page multiplies so that you can follow the sums.

Checking it

It is fair to ask whether forgetting everything else loses the best answer. For short runs we can find out by scoring every story. The page does this for each run you build, up to twelve days.

The grid has one cell per weather per day, and each cell looks at every weather the day before. So the work grows with the number of days times the square of the number of weathers, where trying every story grows by multiplying by the number of weathers for each extra day.

Uses
Decoding error-correcting codes of a kind called convolutional codes was its original setting. It is also widely used in speech recognition, tagging words with their parts of speech, and matching a noisy GPS trail to the roads that were probably driven.
Forward–backward and Baum–Welch
The related algorithms for the chance of the clues, and for learning the tables of chances from examples when you do not know them.
Beam search
When there are too many states even for the grid, keep only the best few in each column. It is faster and no longer guaranteed to find the best.

In short

  • A hidden chain of states produces clues. You see the clues and want the most likely chain.
  • The number of stories doubles, or worse, with each extra step, so scoring them all is out.
  • The best story ending in a state on a given day is built from the best story ending in some state the day before. Remember only that.
  • A grid with one cell per state per step holds those best scores, and remembered back-links recover the whole story.
  • Looking at each step alone misses how the steps fit together.
