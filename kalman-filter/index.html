How much to believe

A sensor reading is never quite right, and a prediction from the last step is never quite right either. A Kalman filter combines the two, leaning towards whichever is more certain, and keeps track of how certain the result is. This page builds that from two bell curves, then runs it on a noisy track, then adds a hidden speed, which is where it earns its keep.

Two guesses, one answer

Suppose you are tracking something along a line. From where it was a moment ago you predict that it is now at about 40, give or take 12. A sensor then says 60, give or take 6. Each is a guess with a spread. We show a guess with uncertainty as a bell curve: the peak is the best value, and a wider curve means less certainty. Move the sliders.

The combined curve sits between the two, nearer the narrower one, and it is narrower than either. Two uncertain guesses together tell you more than one alone. The sums are short. Write variance for the spread squared.

gain K
The prediction’s variance divided by the sum of both variances. It is near 1 when the prediction is vague and the reading is sharp, and near 0 when the reverse is true.
new estimate
The prediction, plus K times the gap between the reading and the prediction. The gap is called the innovation. K says how much of it to believe.
new variance
One minus K, times the prediction’s variance. It is always smaller than the prediction’s.

An equivalent way to see the last line: the combined variance is such that one over it equals one over the first variance plus one over the second. Certainties add up.

The loop

A Kalman filter repeats two steps for every new reading.

Predict
Say where the thing should be now, from where it was. In this section the guess is simply “where it was”. Time has passed and it might have moved, so the variance is made bigger by a fixed amount, the process noise. Uncertainty grows.
Update
A reading arrives. Combine it with the prediction exactly as above. Uncertainty shrinks.

“Typical error” is the root-mean-square distance from the truth, over all the steps shown. The filter beats the raw readings comfortably. You may also notice that the plain average of the last five readings does about as well, and sometimes better. That is fair. For a slow path and steady noise, averaging is a decent filter. What the Kalman filter adds is that it needs no window length to be chosen, it says how uncertain it is, it can use readings whose noise varies, and it can carry a model of the movement, which is the next part. Meanwhile, try these:

  • Tell the filter the sensor is much less noisy than it is. It trusts every reading, so the estimate jitters.
  • Tell it the sensor is much more noisy than it is. It barely believes the readings, so it follows the path late.
  • Turn the wander allowance down. The estimate becomes smooth but slow, because the filter insists that nothing much happens between steps.

Look at the band too. In this simple filter the uncertainty settles to a fixed width. It does not depend on what the readings were, only on how noisy the filter believes the sensor and the movement to be. That is a general property of this kind of filter, and it is why the gain can be worked out in advance.

A hidden speed

Now something moves steadily, say a car on a road, and the sensor reports only its position. The filter above has no idea about speed. Each step it predicts “it is where it was”, and each reading has to drag it forwards, so it always runs behind a steadily moving thing.

The remedy is to track speed as well, even though it is never measured. The state is now a position and a speed. Predicting moves the position on by the speed. Because position and speed are uncertain together, the filter keeps a small table of how they vary: the variance of the position, of the speed, and how the two move together, called the covariance. When a reading says the car is further along than predicted, the covariance is what lets the filter conclude that the speed was probably higher than it thought, and revise the speed too. Speed is learned from positions alone.

Raise the wander allowance for the filter without speed and its lag shrinks, but its estimate gets noisier, because it now believes the readings more. The filter with speed does not need that trade. Try the speed change as well: the filter with speed overshoots briefly and then adjusts, and how fast it adjusts is set by the speed-change allowance.

What it assumes

  • Everything is a bell curve. The filter describes its belief only by a centre and a spread. If the real uncertainty has two humps, such as “it went left or right at the junction”, one bell curve cannot hold it.
  • The movement and the sensors are straight-line relationships. That is what keeps the sums this short. Turning, or a sensor that measures distance and angle, bends them, and needs the variants named below.
  • You must tell it the noise levels. It does not find them out. Wrong numbers give the lag and jitter you saw above.

In short

  • A guess with an uncertainty is a bell curve. Combining two weights each by how certain it is, and the result is more certain than either.
  • The filter repeats predict, which grows the uncertainty, and update, which shrinks it.
  • How much to believe a reading is the gain K, set by the two variances.
  • The noise levels you give the filter decide its behaviour: too trusting is jittery, too doubtful is late.
  • Adding a hidden speed lets the filter follow steady movement without lag, learning the speed from positions alone.
